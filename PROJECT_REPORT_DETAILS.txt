4. ABSTRACT
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, unprovoked seizures that affect 
over 50 million individuals worldwide. The unpredictable onset of seizures causes severe physical risks, 
accidents, psychological distress, and social stigmatization. This project establishes an end-to-end, 
reproducible, and clinically oriented machine learning system for preictal epileptic seizure prediction 
using long-term continuous multichannel scalp electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings from the CHB-MIT 
Scalp EEG Database (Boston Children's Hospital and MIT). 

Unlike seizure detection (which merely identifies ongoing ictal events), seizure prediction identifies 
subtle neurophysiological precursors in the preictal window prior to clinical onset, providing an actionable 
time horizon for timely therapeutic interventions, automated drug delivery, or patient safety warnings. 

The pipeline implements rigorous methodological safeguards against data leakage and spurious artifacts:
1. Canonical Channel Harmonization: Reconciles varying 22–31 channel configurations across recordings into 
   a standardized 18-channel longitudinal bipolar montage (International 10-20 system).
2. Zero-Phase Signal Conditioning: 4th-order zero-phase Butterworth bandpass filtering (0.5–40.0 Hz) via 
   Second-Order Sections (SOS) and zero-phase 50.0 Hz notch filtering (Q=30) to eliminate powerline interference.
3. Robust Window Normalization: Median and Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) scaling with symmetric outlier 
   clipping to [-20, 20] standard units, resisting transient artifacts and electrode disconnections.
4. Leakage-Free Temporal Protocol: 30.0-second non-overlapping windows (7,680 samples @ 256 Hz) with preictal 
   defined as [onset - 35 min, onset - 5 min], a mandatory 5-minute prediction horizon buffer before onset 
   excluded from training, ictal exclusion [onset, offset], and a 30-minute postictal exclusion window.
5. Patient-Independent Splitting: Disjoint patient-level stratification (8 training patients: chb01, chb03, 
   chb05, chb06, chb07, chb08, chb09, chb10; 2 validation patients: chb02, chb11; and 2 strictly held-out 
   test patients: chb04, chb12) with zero recording or window leakage.

Three distinct modeling paradigms are engineered and benchmarked:
- Baseline A: Balanced Random Forest operating on an enhanced 453-dimensional feature space combining 
  time-domain statistics, Hjorth parameters (Activity, Mobility, Complexity), spectral band powers 
  (Delta, Theta, Alpha, Beta, Gamma), relative powers, spectral ratios (Theta/Beta, Delta/Alpha, Slow/Fast), 
  normalized spectral entropy, channel connectivity, and global hemispheric synchrony/asymmetry metrics.
- Baseline B: Deep Multichannel 1D-CNN (Conv1D-BatchNorm-ReLU-MaxPool-Dropout hierarchy) performing direct 
  end-to-end spatial-spectral representation learning on raw arrays (18, 7680).
- Primary Candidate: Hierarchical Spatial-Temporal CNN-LSTM network that extracts window-level spatial features 
  via 1D-CNN and captures sequential temporal transitions across consecutive EEG windows via LSTM.
- Patient-Calibrated Benchmark: Personalized models trained per patient using stratified cross-validation.

On the untouched held-out test patients (chb04 and chb12), the CNN-LSTM candidate achieves 82.65% Sensitivity 
(Recall) and 58.06% Balanced Accuracy, while the Random Forest pipeline achieves 57.25% Balanced Accuracy and 
0.6135 AUROC. In personalized cross-validation across 12 patients, models attain an average of 89.2% Balanced 
Accuracy, 86.8% Sensitivity, 92.6% Specificity, and 0.9664 AUROC. To bridge the gap between window-level scores 
and clinical utility, an event-level alarm simulation with persistence filtering (k=2 consecutive positive 
windows) and a 60-minute post-alarm refractory period is integrated. A full-stack real-time web demonstration 
console provides an interactive 18-channel oscilloscope canvas, multi-model consensus staging, and live telemetry.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1.1 Background & Clinical Context of Epilepsy:
Epilepsy is a chronic, non-communicable neurological condition defined by enduring predisposition to generate 
epileptic seizures. Seizures result from excessive, synchronized electrical neuronal discharges in cortical 
networks. Epilepsy affects children, adults, and the elderly across all demographics, representing approximately 
1% of the world's population. Current clinical mainstays rely primarily on anti-seizure medications (ASMs). 
However, approximately 30% of patients suffer from pharmacoresistant (refractory) epilepsy, where seizures 
persist despite optimal multi-drug regimens. For these patients, uncontrolled seizures pose constant hazards: 
falls, head trauma, burns, drowning, status epilepticus, and SUDEP. 

1.2 Crucial Distinction: Seizure Detection vs. Seizure Prediction:
A critical conceptual error in machine learning literature is conflating detection with prediction:
- Seizure Detection (Ictal Detection): Focuses on classifying EEG during an ongoing seizure event. 
  The onset has already occurred; the patient is actively experiencing clinical manifestations or 
  paroxysmal discharges. While useful for closed-circuit hospital monitoring or retrospective logging, 
  detection arrives too late to avert injury, prepare rescue therapy, or avert the seizure.
- Seizure Prediction (Preictal Forecasting): Aims to detect subtle, micro-structural, non-linear alterations 
  in neural dynamics occurring minutes to tens of minutes prior to seizure onset (the preictal state). 
  Successful prediction affords an actionable therapeutic window (e.g., 5 to 35 minutes) allowing 
  the patient to move to a safe posture, activate neuromodulatory stimulation (e.g., responsive neurostimulation, 
  VNS), or self-administer rapid-acting rescue drugs.

1.3 Project Motivation and Engineering Objectives:
Building a practical, leak-free seizure prediction system poses acute signal-processing and machine-learning 
challenges: non-stationary EEG signals, low signal-to-noise ratio, subject-to-subject variability, extreme class 
imbalance (>95% interictal background), and artifacts from ocular, muscle, and powerline noise. 
The objectives of this project are:
1. Develop an end-to-end reproducible pipeline from raw EDF files to evaluated models.
2. Resolve subtle parser and data corruption bugs that zeroed out preictal labels in prior setups.
3. Establish a standardized 18-channel longitudinal bipolar montage conforming to International 10-20 standards.
4. Implement a leakage-free prediction protocol enforcing preictal, horizon buffer, ictal, and postictal rules.
5. Benchmark three machine learning paradigms under strict patient-independent and patient-calibrated regimes.
6. Provide an event-level alarm simulation and a real-time web telemetry console.

1.4 System Architecture Overview:
The system pipeline operates across six distinct operational layers:
Layer 1: Dataset Audit & Annotation Parsing (CHB-MIT summaries, seizure onset/offset timestamps).
Layer 2: Signal Conditioning & Windowing (18-channel extraction, 0.5-40Hz bandpass, 50Hz notch, robust normalization, 30s slicing).
Layer 3: Dataset Splitting & Serialization (Compressed NPZ archives, metadata CSVs, patient-independent splits).
Layer 4: Feature Engineering & Deep Representation (453-D vectorized feature space, 1D-CNN, CNN-LSTM).
Layer 5: Comparative Evaluation & Alarm Simulation (Window metrics, confusion matrices, persistence filter, FPR/h).
Layer 6: Interactive Deployment Console (Flask web server, REST endpoints, HTML5 multi-channel oscilloscope canvas).

1.5 Clinical Safety Disclaimer and Boundary of Scope:
This codebase is an engineering and scientific research prototype designed to evaluate algorithmic feasibility 
on retrospective data. It does not constitute an approved medical device, diagnostic system, or treatment guide. 
It must not be utilized for live clinical diagnosis or treatment without formal prospective validation, clinical trials, 
and regulatory certification.

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE SURVEY
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.1 Benchmark Foundation & Database Formulation (CHB-MIT Scalp EEG):
The foundation for this project is the CHB-MIT Scalp EEG Database, collected at Boston Children's Hospital in 
collaboration with the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and distributed via PhysioNet (Goldberger et al., 
2000; Shoeb, 2009). The database comprises continuous multi-channel scalp EEG recordings from 24 pediatric and 
young adult subjects with intractable seizures. Across the dataset:
- Total EDF recordings found: 686 files.
- Total unique patients: 24 patients (chb01 through chb24).
- Recordings with annotated seizures: 141 files.
- Total verified seizure events: 198 seizures.
- Seizures with a complete >= 35-minute continuous preictal recording: 91 seizures.
- Sampling frequency: Standardized at 256.0 Hz across all subjects.

2.2 Research Issues and Engineering Problems Identified in Previous Work:
A rigorous code audit of initial preprocessing scripts revealed major defects and methodological flaws 
common in preliminary implementations:

2.2.1 Root Cause Analysis of the "Zero Preictal Windows" Defect:
In the initial project implementation, running preprocessing across subjects yielded tens of thousands 
of windows with 0 preictal labels:
- Original bug results:
  * chb01: 9,730 total windows -> 0 preictal windows.
  * chb02: 8,462 total windows -> 0 preictal windows.
  * chb03: 9,120 total windows -> 0 preictal windows.
Root Cause: The original annotation parser in `parse_summary` utilized the regular expression:
  re.match(r"\s*Seizure\s+\d+\s+Start Time:\s*(\d+)\s+seconds", line, re.I)
In the CHB-MIT annotation text files, when a recording contains exactly one seizure (as is the case for every 
seizure file in chb01, chb02, and chb03), the summary text omits the seizure index digit entirely:
  File Name: chb01_03.edf
  Number of Seizures in File: 1
  Seizure Start Time: 2996 seconds
  Seizure End Time: 3036 seconds
Because the regex mandated `\s+\d+\s+`, it failed to match any single-seizure file, silently missing 40 unnumbered 
seizures. The parsed seizure list was empty `[]`, causing `label_window()` to classify all windows as interictal (0).
Fix Implemented: A flexible regex supporting optional numbering `(?:#?\s*\d+)?` was deployed and verified via unit tests:
  re.match(r"^\s*Seizure(?:\s*#?\s*\d+)?\s+Start\s*Time\s*:\s*(\d+)\s*seconds", line, re.I)

2.2.2 Operating System & Path Duplication Pitfalls (Windows NTFS):
Original data loaders merged glob patterns using:
  list(p.glob("*.edf")) + list(p.glob("*.EDF"))
Because Windows NTFS file systems are case-insensitive, this code pattern duplicated every single recording file, 
artificially inflating dataset size, duplicating samples, and risking data contamination.
Fix Implemented: Case-folding dictionary deduplication based on canonical lowercase file paths.

2.2.3 Channel Montage Inconsistencies & Artifact Contamination:
Across CHB-MIT subjects chb01–chb24, channel counts vary widely between 22, 23, 28, and 31 channels. 
Recordings frequently include non-EEG reference channels (`--0`, `ECG`, `VNS`), duplicate channel names 
with suffixes added by EDF readers (`T8-P8-0` vs `T8-P8-1`), or differing channel orderings.
Fix Implemented: The `select_and_order_channels()` function sanitizes channel strings, strips trailing disambiguation 
suffixes via regex `[-\.]\d+$`, and maps every recording into the standardized 18-channel longitudinal bipolar montage.

2.2.4 Severe Class Imbalance & The "Accuracy Paradox":
Continuous EEG in clinical monitoring is over 95% interictal. A naive classifier predicting the majority class 
achieves >95% accuracy while completely failing to predict seizures. Evaluating models strictly using raw 
classification accuracy is scientifically invalid. Balanced accuracy, AUROC, PR-AUC, and clinical alarm simulation 
must be employed.

2.2.5 Data Leakage in EEG Machine Learning Literature:
Many published studies randomly shuffle 30-second windows across a patient cohort into train and test folds. 
Because consecutive windows from the same subject share temporal background and spatial impedance characteristics, 
random window shuffling introduces massive information leakage, yielding unrealistically high reported accuracies 
(98-99%) that collapse to chance when deployed on unseen patients. To address this, strict patient-independent 
cohort splitting is mandatory.

CHAPTER 3: PROPOSED SYSTEM (AI-BASED EPILEPTIC SEIZURE PREDICTION USING CHB-MIT SCALP EEG)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.1 Theoretical Background, Concepts, Methodology, Design & Modeling:

3.1.1 Continuous Recording Timeline & Windowing Protocol:
The system applies a strict, non-overlapping windowing and temporal labeling protocol:
- Sampling Frequency ($f_s$): 256.0 Hz.
- Window Length ($T_w$): 30.0 seconds ($N = 256 \times 30 = 7,680$ samples per channel).
- Window Stride: 30.0 seconds (disjoint windows; no artificial overlap).
- Preictal Definition: Window strictly contained in $[t_{onset} - 2100\text{ s}, t_{onset} - 300\text{ s}]$ 
  (35 minutes to 5 minutes prior to electrographic onset). Labeled as Class 1.
- Prediction Horizon / Intervention Buffer: The interval $[t_{onset} - 300\text{ s}, t_{onset}]$ (final 5 minutes 
  before onset) is completely EXCLUDED from training and testing. This guarantees that any positive prediction 
  provides at least 5 minutes of advance warning for clinical response.
- Ictal Period Exclusion: The seizure interval $[t_{onset}, t_{offset}]$ is strictly excluded.
- Postictal Exclusion: The interval $[t_{offset}, t_{offset} + 1800\text{ s}]$ (30 minutes following offset) is 
  excluded to prevent post-ictal slowing or suppression patterns from contaminating baseline interictal data.
- Interictal Baseline: Clean EEG outside all preictal, horizon, ictal, and postictal intervals. Labeled as Class 0.

3.1.2 Signal Conditioning & Preprocessing Pipeline:
- Zero-Phase Bandpass Filtering: 4th-order Butterworth digital filter with passband 0.5 Hz – 40.0 Hz, 
  executed using Second-Order Sections (`scipy.signal.sosfiltfilt`) to prevent phase distortion while 
  suppressing DC offset, baseline drift (<0.5 Hz), and high-frequency muscle/EMG noise (>40 Hz).
- Zero-Phase Powerline Notch Filtering: 50.0 Hz notch filter with quality factor $Q = 30.0$, 
  executed using `scipy.signal.iirnotch` followed by forward-backward `scipy.signal.filtfilt`.

3.1.3 Canonical 18-Channel Longitudinal Bipolar Montage Harmonization:
All EEG recordings are harmonized to the standard International 10-20 longitudinal bipolar montage (Double Banana):
  Channel 1: FP1-F7 (Left anterior temporal)
  Channel 2: F7-T7   (Left mid temporal)
  Channel 3: T7-P7   (Left posterior temporal)
  Channel 4: P7-O1   (Left occipito-temporal)
  Channel 5: FP1-F3  (Left anterior parasagittal)
  Channel 6: F3-C3   (Left mid parasagittal)
  Channel 7: C3-P3   (Left centro-parietal)
  Channel 8: P3-O1   (Left parieto-occipital)
  Channel 9: FP2-F4  (Right anterior parasagittal)
  Channel 10: F4-C4  (Right mid parasagittal)
  Channel 11: C4-P4  (Right centro-parietal)
  Channel 12: P4-O2  (Right parieto-occipital)
  Channel 13: FP2-F8 (Right anterior temporal)
  Channel 14: F8-T8  (Right mid temporal)
  Channel 15: T8-P8  (Right posterior temporal)
  Channel 16: P8-O2  (Right occipito-temporal)
  Channel 17: FZ-CZ  (Midline frontal-central)
  Channel 18: CZ-PZ  (Midline central-parietal)

3.1.4 Robust Normalization & Dynamic Range Control:
Rather than standard z-score normalization (which is severely skewed by high-amplitude transient artifacts), 
the system implements window-level Median and Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) normalization:
  $$\hat{X}_c = \frac{X_c - \text{median}(X_c)}{1.4826 \times \text{MAD}(X_c) + \epsilon}$$
where $\text{MAD}(X_c) = \text{median}(|X_c - \text{median}(X_c)|)$ and $1.4826$ scales MAD to the standard deviation 
of a normal distribution. The normalized signals are symmetrically clamped to $[-20.0, 20.0]$ standard units.

3.1.5 Patient-Independent Splitting Manifest (Zero-Leakage Cohorts):
To rigorously evaluate model generalization across unseen patients, a disjoint 12-patient cohort is partitioned:
- Training Cohort (8 patients): chb01, chb03, chb05, chb06, chb07, chb08, chb09, chb10.
  * 60 recordings; 11,105 total windows (2,279 preictal, 8,826 interictal).
- Validation Cohort (2 patients): chb02, chb11.
  * 9 recordings; 833 total windows (215 preictal, 618 interictal). Used for checkpointing and threshold tuning.
- Untouched Held-Out Test Cohort (2 patients): chb04, chb12.
  * 17 recordings; 2,626 total windows (463 preictal, 2,163 interictal). Completely unseen during training.

3.1.6 Baseline A: 453-Dimensional Feature Engineering & Random Forest:
For each 30-second window ($18 \times 7680$), a vectorized feature extraction routine computes 25 features 
per channel plus 3 global synchronization features:
1. Time-Domain (6 features/channel): Mean, Standard Deviation, Variance, RMS, Peak-to-Peak Amplitude, Energy.
2. Hjorth Parameters (3 features/channel):
   - Activity: $\text{var}(y(t))$
   - Mobility: $\sqrt{\text{var}(y'(t)) / \text{var}(y(t))}$
   - Complexity: $\text{Mobility}(y'(t)) / \text{Mobility}(y(t))$
3. Welch Spectral Band Powers (5 features/channel): Delta (0.5–4Hz), Theta (4–8Hz), Alpha (8–12Hz), Beta (12–30Hz), Gamma (30–40Hz).
4. Relative Band Powers (5 features/channel): Band power normalized by total power in 0.5–40 Hz.
5. Band Power Ratios (3 features/channel):
   - Theta / Beta ratio
   - Delta / Alpha ratio
   - Slow / Fast wave ratio: $(\text{Theta} + \text{Alpha}) / (\text{Beta} + \text{Gamma})$
6. Normalized Spectral Entropy (1 feature/channel): Shannon entropy of normalized PSD, divided by $\log_2(K)$.
7. Total Spectral Power (1 feature/channel): Total power in 0.5–40 Hz.
8. Channel Connectivity (1 feature/channel): Mean Pearson cross-correlation coefficient with all other 17 channels.
Subtotal per channel: 25 features $\times$ 18 channels = 450 features.
Global Features (3 features):
- Global Cross-Channel Synchrony Mean: Mean of upper triangular off-diagonal correlation matrix.
- Global Cross-Channel Synchrony Std: Standard deviation of upper triangular correlation matrix.
- Hemispheric Energy Asymmetry: $(E_{left} - E_{right}) / (E_{left} + E_{right} + \epsilon)$, 
  where $E_{left}$ is mean energy of channels 0–7 and $E_{right}$ is mean energy of channels 8–15.
Total Feature Dimension: $450 + 3 = 453$ features per window.
Classifier: Scikit-learn Pipeline with `RobustScaler` and `RandomForestClassifier` (100 estimators, max depth 15, balanced class weights).

3.1.7 Baseline B: Deep Multichannel 1D-CNN Architecture:
End-to-end representation learning on raw arrays $(B, 18, 7680)$:
- Block 1: Conv1D(18 -> 32, kernel=7, stride=2, pad=3) -> BatchNorm1D(32) -> ReLU -> MaxPool1D(4) -> Dropout(0.3)
- Block 2: Conv1D(32 -> 64, kernel=5, stride=2, pad=2) -> BatchNorm1D(64) -> ReLU -> MaxPool1D(4) -> Dropout(0.3)
- Block 3: Conv1D(64 -> 128, kernel=3, stride=1, pad=1) -> BatchNorm1D(128) -> ReLU -> AdaptiveAvgPool1D(1)
- Classification Head: Flatten -> Dropout(0.3) -> Linear(128 -> 64) -> ReLU -> Dropout(0.3) -> Linear(64 -> 1)
- Loss: `BCEWithLogitsLoss(pos_weight)` with Adam optimizer (lr=0.001, weight_decay=1e-4).

3.1.8 Primary Candidate: Hierarchical Spatial-Temporal CNN-LSTM Architecture:
Processes chronological sequences of consecutive windows $(B, T, 18, 7680)$ with sequence length $T=5$ (or $T=10$):
- Spatial Encoder (1D-CNN):
  * Conv1D(18 -> 32, kernel=7, stride=2, pad=3) -> BatchNorm1D(32) -> ReLU -> MaxPool1D(4) -> Dropout(0.3)
  * Conv1D(32 -> 64, kernel=5, stride=2, pad=2) -> BatchNorm1D(64) -> ReLU -> AdaptiveAvgPool1D(16)
  * Output per window: $64 \times 16 = 1,024$ spatial features.
- Temporal Sequence Modeling (LSTM):
  * Input size: 1,024; Hidden size: 64; Batch-first; Single layer.
  * Last hidden state $h_T \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$ passed through Dropout(0.3) and Linear(64 -> 1).

3.1.9 Patient-Calibrated / Personalized Stratified Modeling:
In clinical neurotechnology (e.g., responsive neurostimulators), seizure foci and baseline EEG characteristics 
are highly patient-specific. Using `train_personalized.py`, 5-fold stratified cross-validation was conducted 
for each of the 12 patients individually using `RobustScaler` and L2-regularized logistic regression / balanced RF.

3.1.10 Event-Level Alarm Simulation & Clinical Persistence Filter:
In continuous clinical monitoring, a 1% false alarm rate produces unacceptable alert fatigue. To convert 
window predictions into actionable alarms:
- Alarm Persistence Filter ($k = 2$): An alarm is triggered ONLY if the model outputs positive predictions 
  in at least $k=2$ consecutive windows (1 full minute of sustained preictal evidence).
- Refractory Period (60 minutes): Following an alarm trigger or seizure event, subsequent alarms are inhibited 
  for 60 minutes to prevent alert cascading.
- Event Metrics: Event Sensitivity (seizures preceded by an alarm within 35–5 min), False Prediction Rate per hour (FPR/h), 
  and Mean Warning Lead Time.

4.4 Comparative Synthesis Across Modeling Paradigms:
1. Patient-Independent vs. Patient-Calibrated Discrepancy:
   The substantial drop from personalized AUROC (0.9664) to patient-independent AUROC (0.6135) confirms 
   that scalp EEG preictal dynamics are largely idiosyncratic. Seizure onset zones (frontal, temporal, occipital), 
   paroxysmal frequency shifts, and propagation pathways differ fundamentally between individual epileptic brains. 
   Deployable clinical systems (such as the NeuroPace RNS) succeed because they calibrate to individual patient baselines.
2. CNN-LSTM Temporal Sensitivity:
   The CNN-LSTM candidate achieves the highest Sensitivity (82.65%) on held-out test patients, demonstrating 
   that temporal sequence modeling over consecutive windows captures the cumulative buildup of preictal dynamics. 
   However, its lower specificity (33.46%) highlights the necessity of downstream alarm persistence filtering.
3. Random Forest Spatial-Spectral Balance:
   The Random Forest model operating on handcrafted features exhibits the most balanced performance in the 
   patient-independent regime (57.25% Balanced Accuracy, 73.46% Specificity, 0.6135 AUROC), showing that engineered 
   cross-channel correlation and spectral power ratios offer superior cross-subject stability compared to naive 1D-CNNs.

4.5 Event-Level Alarm Simulation & Clinical Trade-Offs:
Window-level accuracy is insufficient for assessing clinical utility. In continuous monitoring:
- A false positive rate of 5% at the 30-second window level equates to 6 false alarms per hour (FPR/h = 6.0), 
  which induces severe alert fatigue and causes patients to abandon monitoring devices.
- By enforcing the persistence filter ($k=2$), isolated noise spikes and transient movement artifacts are rejected.
- Adding the 60-minute post-alarm refractory period prevents repeated alarm cascading during the preictal and postictal periods.
- Event-level evaluation on chb01_03 successfully predicted seizure onset with over 15 minutes of advance lead time.

5.1 Conclusion of Engineering Findings:
This project delivers a complete, leak-free, reproducible machine learning engineering framework for epileptic 
seizure prediction on the CHB-MIT Scalp EEG Database:
1. Resolved critical unnumbered-seizure parser bugs that previously caused zero preictal windows.
2. Standardized heterogeneous recordings into the canonical 18-channel longitudinal bipolar montage (International 10-20).
3. Enforced a leak-free temporal prediction protocol with strict preictal, horizon buffer, ictal, and postictal definitions.
4. Benchmarked Random Forest (453 features), 1D-CNN, and CNN-LSTM models under both patient-independent and 
   patient-calibrated experimental regimes.
5. Demonstrated that personalized calibration delivers exceptional fidelity (89.2% Balanced Accuracy, 0.9664 AUROC), 
   while patient-independent modeling requires sequence modeling and persistence filtering to suppress false alarms.
6. Deployed an interactive full-stack web demonstration console featuring real-time 18-channel waveform rendering.

5.2 Summary of Methodological Contributions:
- Complete elimination of data leakage via disjoint patient splits.
- Vectorized 453-dimensional feature extraction routine processing 18 channels simultaneously.
- Implementation of event-level alarm persistence filtering to bridge the gap between window-level accuracy 
  and real-world clinical feasibility.

5.3 Future Scope & Technological Enhancements:
1. Transfer Learning & Self-Supervised Pretraining: Pretrain spatial-temporal transformer architectures (e.g., 
   masked autoencoders on large unlabeled continuous EEG archives like the Temple University Hospital EEG corpus) 
   prior to fine-tuning on patient-specific seizure recordings.
2. Edge Hardware Acceleration: Quantize the 1D-CNN and CNN-LSTM architectures into INT8/FP16 models for deployment 
   on ultra-low-power neuromorphic or wearable edge microcontrollers (e.g., ARM Cortex-M55, STM32, or Apple Neural Engine).
3. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs): Explicitly model scalp electrode topography as dynamic graph structures, 
   where edges represent instantaneous phase synchronization across cortical regions.
4. Prospective Clinical Validation: Validate the trained models on ambulatory continuous EEG datasets from patients 
   undergoing routine daily activities, evaluating true False Alarm Rates per 24 hours.
